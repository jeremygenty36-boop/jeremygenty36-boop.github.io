Master's internship · UMR Silva, AgroParisTech, Nancy · 2026

Climate extremes and tree mortality

Key findings

  • Same performance, different reading. The 5 datasets explain mortality equally well (AUC 0.758 to 0.763). But for 6 of the 8 species, the most important climate extreme changes with the dataset used.
  • Datasets agree on intensity, not on frequency. For extreme temperatures the datasets are highly correlated (R² 0.85 to 0.87); for heatwaves, cold spells and drought, much less so (R² 0.14 to 0.6).
  • Climate remains secondary. Extremes account for 8.2% of the explained mortality on average (from 1.1% for beech to 26% for birch); stand structure dominates. A 1 km resolution does not improve the models at the scale of France.
  • Extremes or mean climate? Extreme-event indices do not predict mortality better than mean climate: the AUCs of both approaches differ by at most 0.003, and mean climate even weighs slightly more (9.0% vs 8.2%).
Method, figures and documents

Climate datasets do not produce the same extremes. Does that choice change what we conclude about forest mortality? I computed 6 extreme-event indices (heat, cold, drought) with 5 climate datasets, two of which I downscaled from 8-10 km to 1 km, then modelled the mortality of 8 tree species on 21,989 National Forest Inventory plots (GLMs in R, BIC selection, 1,000 validation splits).

How this fits the literature

To my knowledge, few studies focus specifically on the role of climate extremes in forest mortality; the closest is Carletti et al. (2026), on silver fir and Norway spruce. Despite different methods, some trends agree: in spruce the most explanatory variables are also heat-related, and in fir both studies find a role for cold spells (5.5% of importance in my study vs 2.5%). Extremes weigh much more in theirs, however (up to 22% for fir and 29% for spruce): that study focused on pure stands and included autumn extremes, which mine did not.

Next steps

  • Resolution and selection: understand why more indices enter the models at 1 km.
  • Autumn extremes: include them, since they matter in Carletti et al. (2026).
  • Cold and pests: test the indirect effect of cold through spruce bark beetles, using biotic data.
  • Finer data: examine Météo-France 2.5 km products and feed process-based models such as SurEau.

Quality assurance. I published the full processing pipeline openly, with a 17-item register (ERRATA) documenting every error found afterwards and its effect on the results: none invalidates the conclusions, the most exposed being the low weight of drought. Outliers were kept and explained rather than discarded.

Interactive map · 2026

Climate extremes in France: 1979-2024

The hottest days of the year have warmed by 0.8 °C per decade on average in France since 1979, and heatwaves rose from 1.4 to 5 per year (May to September, 1979-1988 vs 2015-2024 averages).

Method, figures and documents

Six heat, cold and drought indices (including heatwaves and cold spells), year by year, across mainland France. I recomputed the indices from the European E-OBS observations (11 GB of daily data) with the method of my internship, then built an interactive map: year-by-year animation, 10-year extremes, trends with their significance, and the time series of any location on click.

The map connects to my internship: for each index it shows its weight in the mortality models of 8 tree species and each species' response curve, and a second layer maps the mortality observed in the National Forest Inventory by département.

First-year Master's project · Landscape ecology and GIS, University of Orléans · 2023-2024 · with Pierre Sergent

Mapping the summer 2022 wildfires in the Landes de Gascogne forest

At La Teste-de-Buch we measured 3,538 ha burnt, against 3,500 ha reported in the press, and 12,737 ha around Hostens. Overall accuracy of 93.8% by area.

Method, figures and documents

From two Sentinel-2 images (September 2021 and September 2022), we mapped the forests burnt by the two large fires of summer 2022: before/after vegetation index (NDVI) difference, forest mask from the OSO land-cover map, thresholding into three probability classes, sieving and vectorisation in QGIS and Orfeo ToolBox, then validation by photo-interpretation of 50 polygons per class.

First-year Master's · Phytosociology and plant ecology, University of Orléans · 2023-2024 · with Claire Bellin

Phytosociology of meadows and dry grasslands in the Loiret

11 plant communities identified. The two main groups differ in soil fertility and moisture (Mann-Whitney, p < 0.001), not in pH. A limitation we acknowledged: the classification structure remains weak (mean silhouette of 0.08).

Method, figures and documents

From 258 vegetation relevés at three sites in the Loiret (Saint-Mesmin, Tavers, Guilly), we grouped the relevés by hierarchical clustering (chord distance, Ward's method), chose the number of groups with silhouette and OptimClass, assigned each group to a described syntaxon (association or alliance), then compared groups with ecograms and a synoptic table to propose suitable management.

First-year Master's internship · Gembloux Agro-Bio Tech (University of Liège) and CRA-W, Lipomme BIO project · 2024

Does a biological fungicide harm beneficial insects in orchards?

1,813 insects from 31 families, with no community difference between treatments (p = 0.276): weather matters far more than treatment. I discuss the protocol's biases, such as insects moving between neighbouring trees.

Method, figures and documents

Mycosubtilin, an antifungal molecule of bacterial origin, is being studied as an alternative to copper and sulphur against apple scab. I compared insects caught in yellow traps under four treatments (water, mycosubtilin, sulphur and copper, GAWI method), on 4 replicates of 10 apple trees over 18 weeks: family-level identification, principal component analysis and Kruskal-Wallis test in R.
